## The current state of AI 
As of now (Nov 22nd, 2026), frontier AI models have achieved incredible feats including the resolution of [Generalized Riemann Hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Riemann_hypothesis) (by Levent Alpöge with Claude), the refutation of the [Exponential Time Hypothesis](https://courses.cs.washington.edu/courses/cse599i/24sp/papers/ImpagliazzoPaturi_2001_jcss.pdf) (ETH) (by OpenAI), the resolution of the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem (by OpenAI), as well as reshaping the paradigms and workflows of modern software engineering. 

## What AI can do, and How humans can contribute
If I were to use one single adjective to describe the tasks AI models are being excellent at, I would use the word "benchmarkable". For instance, almost every single statement in mathematics can be translated into some program in a "programming language" called [Lean](https://lean-lang.org/), and the correctness can be checked by compiling the program on machines to see if it contains errors. Essentially, this "formal verification" process is performed through traversing the logical dependency at a large scale. If humans did not possess such a mechanism to formally verify the correctness of a math theorem, we would be staring at a 250,000-page proof of Generalized Riemann hypothesis and having no idea where the verification process should start. Similarly in Software Engineering, code writers can verify the correctness by various ways of testing (unit testing, property-based testing, etc.), which gives the best benchmark for AI model training by the problems' nature. 

However, there are still things that AI is not currently being good at, despite their fast-paced evolution and miraculous achievements in multiple areas of science. If you followed along with my arguments, you would know that I hold the opinion that AI models are not doing well on "non-benchmarkable" things. One primary example I would always mention whenever I was discussing AI with someone, is the fact that AI-generated code is unreadable unless you did some careful harness engineering and prompting. Some common patterns include over-protection on edge cases, bad code refactoring which leads to poor maintainability. We can never overemphasize the importance of maintainability under a large scale collaboration context, as well as development of high stake systems that are sensitive and critical. This is exactly where human knowledge and experience still thrive. Due to the context window limitation, a fresh-new code assistant session can only receive the current state of repository and human prompt as input, it has no idea what the non-functional requirements (potential scalability, consistency versus availability preference, etc.) are, which is very likely going to produce something that does not fit in the current engineering context. In such scenarios, a consistent and coherent system development still requires the knowledge and experiences that are passed down through human meetings, engineering-decision makings, etc.

Another observation is that AI models often lack a sense of beauty within things, or at least their sense of beauty is badly aligned with that of humans. I will illustrate this by arguing from both the programming perspective and mathematics perspective. From a programming perspective, bad control flow structure has always been one of the most notorious problems for vibe coding. Moreover, code smells such as duplicated code, gigantic class definitions and functions, etc. also make pure vibe coding unacceptable for a disciplined engineering collaboration. Those are both about code organization, and engineering empathy to code reviewers and maintainers, a.k.a. a taste of beautiful design, and it is not about functional correctness (Claude /goal can almost always give you some runnable product, with a sufficient amount of tokens). From a mathematics perspective, I would like to cite the most recent news about Riemann hypothesis as an example. There are about 250,000 pages of natural language proof that is almost IMPOSSIBLE for any human being to process. Broadly speaking, the traditional workflow in math research has been a loop of tool development to problem tackling, e.g., [étale cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89tale_cohomology) to [Weil Conjectures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjectures), and almost always it turns out that those tools can be applied to some other problems. Take [Fermat's Last Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_Last_Theorem), for example. Its resolution took hundreds of years and led to the development of numerous tools in number theory. But the current development of AI for Math has never announced any new theories or tools that came out of the process of solving those hard problems. Every single headline news about AI for Math has always been "some big name problem just got solved", and it was never "a new tool has been established that can have an impact on math development". If you have really read some of those OpenAI Math [papers](https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main) released in October, you would notice that a lot of the proofs rely on some "high order terms" magically cancelling each other, just like Anthropic's refutation of [Jacobian conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_conjecture#Counterexample_for_n_%3E_2). The polynomials with perfect cancelling structure form a zero-measure set in high dimensions, which indicates that there must be some hidden structures that were undiscovered within. AI models managed to solve the problems (which is indeed very impressive), but there is still a large gap between their proofs and real human insights. This also falls into my argument that AI models lack a sense of beauty, for they did not do a good job in  establishing the connections between problems, tools and patterns. On the other hand, a huge amount of upstream proofs requires a huge amount of downstream processing and understanding, and in my opinion, that is exactly the directions that a lot of researchers are switching into. From the people I have talked to within the field (whether it is pure math or TCS), they are actively using AI on a daily basis, whether it is just literature review, or learning new stuff, or attacking problems. And it is almost certain that the current academia systems including both the PhD degree programs and the credit systems are outdated, and changes are required in order to adapt the factor of AI. 

## How I would adapt AI in the foreseeable future
Even though AI tools are super powerful and excel in a wide range of areas, they are still a type of tools in essence (at least at this time of history). There are a few things that are irreplaceable by their nature, no matter how strong those AI models are. As a technical person with Math & CS dual background, my take on this would be undoubtedly biased to some extent. Opinions are my own, and I am open to discussing this with you!
### Interpersonal Communication (Efficiently & Professionally)
As of right now, it does not seem like AI can replace any human being's social appearance and their interpersonal connection. As AI is gradually taking the task of dealing with intellectual complexity from human, along with their huge amount of outputs, it is more and more important to find valuable signals, and convey your ideas to other people. For example, when you are completing a coding task with an agent, it would take much less time to finish it compared to the pre-AI era, and it would be more important to let other people know your intent and discuss design choices in this coding task, since getting a runnable product is no longer a time bound task. Another thing some people like to underestimate in technical communication is "communication empathy", in a sense that the speaker should establish a shared prerequisite knowledge with the people they are talking to, before diving deep into anything. Just like delivering a lecture, if the speaker does not know the knowledge from your audience, their lecture would be of less signal to the audience, which does not make the speaker a good lecturer.

### Thinking from First Principles
One of the things that I got lots of benefit from my previous math competition experiences was thinking from First Principles. The ability to break down a complicated problem into several attackable subproblems or common sense knowledge is a very valuable skill even in the age of AI. I draw this conclusion based on the assumption that in the foreseeable future, those competitive resources are still allocated by some criterion where one's intellectual horsepower plays a large part. The best way to demonstrate this ability is by problem solving or breaking down a big problem into reasonable pieces and elaborating on the thought process, which makes thinking from first principles very useful and important. 

### System Architecture Mental Modelling
It seems to me that in the age of AI, people's attention has shifted on the spectrum from a low level, high granularity area to a high level, low granularity area. For example, instead of paying attention to every single line of code, you might be more interested in the functionality of the component, how it interplays with other components (of course the ability of going into super low level is still of great importance, otherwise a lot of bad design decisions would be made). This led to my last topic of this post which is establishing a mental model from a system point of view. There are a lot of extremely complicated systems in the world, for example, our brains, computers, the universe, financial markets, etc. and it is very hard for one to understand every single thing down to their atoms. My favorite area in mathematics is the study of algebra. I genuinely enjoy how people build objects using basic categorical operations like taking direct limits, taking coproducts, etc. and study their relations on different levels. My ability of probing and understanding a complicated system has been trained a lot through my study in algebra-related topics, it trained me even better than some common computer science system courses like [Operating Systems](https://web.eecs.umich.edu/~nham/eecs482ss20/) and [Distributed Systems](https://cse.engin.umich.edu/academics/course-resources/cse-course-info/#:~:text=EECS%20491%3A%20Introduction%20to%20Distributed%20systems) (those are very interesting and I would definitely recommend studying them too!) Sometimes it seems to me that in an engineering artifact, there are only a few design choices that actually determine whether this is excellent work or mediocre work. In order to make those choices reasonably, it is very crucial to obtain a solid understanding of the stuff you are actually working on. System complexity will not be automatically consumed by AI models, if one is using agents without real system thinking, they will eventually pay the "complexity debt" somewhere in the future.
